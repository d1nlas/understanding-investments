This resource is designed for a student meeting investment finance for the first time. It explains the vocabulary before the equations, connects each chapter to the same underlying financial story, and uses worked problems to turn formulas into decisions.

The course in one sentence: a firm/investor moves money through time, values the resulting cash flows, chooses a return appropriate to risk, and manages the exposures that can make those cash flows uncertain.

Before you touch a formula

1 · A payment is attached to a date
₹100 today, ₹100 in one year and ₹100 in five years are not the same economic object.
2 · Put cash flows on one timeline
Use compounding to move money forward; discounting to move it backward.
3 · Value is a decision
Projects, bonds and stocks are all streams of future cash flows that need to be valued.
4 · Risk changes the required return
A riskier cash-flow stream generally needs a higher required return.
5 · Portfolios change risk through co-movement
Correlation explains why combining assets can reduce total volatility.
6 · Derivatives reshape exposure
Find what hurts your existing position, then choose the offsetting position.

The fictional case that runs through the whole resource

Meet Arclight Mobility. A growing electric-mobility company must decide what to do with its cash, evaluate a new factory, raise financing, understand the value of its shares, manage a portfolio, and hedge future commodity and currency exposures. Every unit returns to Arclight so the ideas feel like different steps in the same financial system.

The chain: cash today → time value → project NPV → financing → bonds → equity valuation → risk/required return → portfolio → hedging → options/swaps/CDS → investor decision.

Finance from zero

This section exists so you never have to guess what a symbol or basic term means.

Asset

Something of economic value owned or controlled; the course distinguishes real assets (factories, IP, inventory, brand) from financial assets (cash, securities).

Liability

A claim against the firm; debt is a liability and gives debt investors a fixed claim.

Equity

Ownership claim on the firm. Equity investors receive the residual claim after other claims.

Bond

A security obligating the issuer to make specified payments to the bondholder.

Stock / common share

Ownership in a corporation; dividends are uncertain and shareholders have voting rights.

Cash flow

Money entering or leaving at a specified time.

Present value (PV)

Value today of a future cash flow after discounting.

Future value (FV)

Amount to which a current amount grows after earning a return for a period.

Required return

Return investors demand given time and risk; this often acts as the discount rate.

Discount rate

The return used to convert future cash flows into present value.

Yield to maturity (YTM)

Bond discount rate that equates price to PV of promised cash flows.

Duration

Cash-flow-weighted timing measure that helps explain bond price sensitivity to rates.

Beta

Sensitivity of an asset’s return to market returns.

Correlation

Measure of how two asset returns move together.

Premium / discount bond

Premium: market price above face value. Discount: market price below face value.

Notional principal

Reference amount used to calculate derivative payments; in a plain-vanilla interest-rate swap it is normally not exchanged.

Maturity

Date/period when a debt instrument ends and face value is repaid, or when a derivative expires.

UNIT 1

Start with one question: what is money worth at another time?

Start with one question: what is money worth at another time?

Where this comes from: This is the engine underneath almost the entire course. When you later value a project, bond or stock, you are mostly applying the same time-value logic to a different cash-flow story.
Arclight case: Arclight Mobility has ₹10 lakh sitting in the bank. It can (A) keep the cash, (B) invest in a machine today that produces cash later, or (C) return the cash to shareholders. Finance begins by asking what each stream of future cash is worth today.

Words you need before symbols

Money

A rupee is useful because it can be spent or invested. So a rupee available now has an opportunity that a future rupee does not have: it can earn a return during the waiting period.

Cash flow

A cash flow is simply money entering or leaving at a particular time. “+₹50,000 in Year 2” is a future cash flow; “−₹100,000 today” is an initial outflow.

Return / rate (r)

In these formulas, r is the rate of return required for the investment. It is the rate at which money grows when you move it forward or the rate used to discount it when you move it backward. The course also calls r a required return/capitalisation rate/cost of equity in the stock section.

Time (t)

t tells you how many periods separate today from the cash flow. If interest is annual and the cash flow is three years away, t=3. If interest is monthly, t must be measured in months.

C

C usually means a recurring cash payment, such as a coupon or annuity payment. In a perpetuity/annuity formula, C means “the amount paid each period.” It is not a universal symbol for one specific thing across finance; always look at the context.

g

g means growth rate. In the Gordon model, g is the rate at which dividends are assumed to grow every period forever.

PV and FV

PV is present value: what a future amount is worth today. FV is future value: what today’s amount becomes after earning a return.

Build the idea

1. The financial manager is allocating capital

The supplied lecture frames finance as decision-making under time and risk—not accounting for its own sake. The firm raises cash, invests it, generates cash, then either reinvests or returns cash to investors.

What should trigger this in your head: The three decisions are investment (where money goes), financing (where the money comes from / debt vs equity), and dividend (when owners receive cash).
Common wrong turn: A project question is usually an investment decision. Debt/equity choice is financing. “Invest or pay out?” is dividend policy.
2. Why value, not just profit?

Profit can look high today while future value is being destroyed. The lecture explicitly contrasts one-period accounting profit with forward-looking value based on future discounted cash flows.

What should trigger this in your head: This is why finance asks what the whole future stream is worth rather than stopping at this year’s profit.
Common wrong turn: A statement like “profit increased” is not enough to conclude value increased.
3. Future value: moving money forward

At 10%, ₹100 becomes ₹110 after one year. In Year 2 you earn 10% on ₹110, not the original ₹100, so it becomes ₹121. That second-period interest on interest is compounding.

What should trigger this in your head: Words like “after 5 years”, “terminal value” or “how much will it grow to?” signal forward movement.
Common wrong turn: Check units: if r is annual, t is years. If r is monthly, t is months.
4. Present value: moving money backward

If someone promises ₹110 one year from now and you can earn 10% elsewhere, you need only ₹100 today to create that same ₹110. Therefore the promise is worth ₹100 today at that required return.

What should trigger this in your head: The discount rate is the return you could demand from comparable capital. PV lets you compare cash flows that happen at different dates.
Common wrong turn: “Worth today” → discount. Do not use FV when the question asks for a current value.
5. Compounding frequency: APR vs EAR

A 12% nominal annual quote with monthly compounding is not the same as 12% actually earned over a year. A 1% monthly rate gives 12% APR but 12.68% EAR.

What should trigger this in your head: The course tests periodic-rate conversion and the idea that more frequent compounding increases the effective annual return when the nominal APR is fixed and positive.
Common wrong turn: Never put an annual rate directly into a monthly formula without converting it.
6. Regular cash-flow patterns

A perpetuity is equal cash forever. A growing perpetuity grows forever. An annuity is equal cash for a finite number of periods. The shortcut formulas are simply compressed versions of the general PV formula.

What should trigger this in your head: The real skill is recognising the stream before choosing the shortcut.
Common wrong turn: Ask: how many payments? equal? growing? when is the first payment?
7. Nominal vs real

Nominal cash flows include inflation. Real cash flows are stated in purchasing-power terms. The discount rate must match the cash-flow basis. The exact Fisher relationship connects nominal, real and inflation rates.

What should trigger this in your head: This prevents double-counting or omitting inflation.
Common wrong turn: Nominal CF + real rate, or real CF + nominal rate, is inconsistent.
8. CAGR is annualised growth, not total growth

A move from 100 to 200 is a 100% cumulative gain, but not 10% per year over ten years. CAGR answers: “What constant annual rate would turn the start into the finish?”

What should trigger this in your head: This prevents a common intuition error when a question gives beginning value, ending value and multiple years.
Common wrong turn: Do not divide total percentage gain by years unless the problem explicitly asks for simple average—not CAGR.

Formula cards — read the English before the symbols

FV
FV = PV(1+r)^t — PV = amount today; r = return per period; t = number of periods.
PV
PV = FV/(1+r)^t — FV = future cash flow; r = required return per period; t = periods until payment.
EAR
EAR = (1+APR/n)^n − 1 — APR = quoted annual rate; n = compounding periods per year.
Perpetuity
PV = C/r — C = constant payment each period; r = required return.
Growing perpetuity
PV = C/(r−g) — C = next period payment; r = required return; g = constant growth rate. Requires r>g.
Annuity
PV = (C/r)[1−1/(1+r)^N] — C = payment each period; r = rate per period; N = number of payments.
CAGR
CAGR=(Ending/Beginning)^(1/t)−1 — converts total change into a constant annual growth rate.
Fisher
1+r_nominal=(1+r_real)(1+π) — π = inflation rate.

Worked applications: problem → reasoning → calculation → interpretation

Arclight’s first valuation problem
Problem statement

Arclight can spend ₹500,000 today on a machine that will produce ₹605,000 exactly two years from today. The required annual return is 10%. Is the future ₹605,000 worth ₹500,000 today?

1
We are comparing money at two dates, so bring the future cash flow back to today.
2
PV = 605,000/(1.10)^2.
3
(1.10)^2 = 1.21.
4
PV = 605,000/1.21 = ₹500,000.
5
So the two alternatives are economically equivalent at exactly 10%.
What the calculation actually means: The machine has not “created ₹105,000 of today’s value”; it has generated exactly the return required on ₹500,000.
Compounding really changes the story
Problem statement

Arclight invests ₹250,000 at 8% per year for five years. What amount will it have at the end?

1
This is money moving forward, so use FV.
2
PV = ₹250,000; r=8%=0.08; t=5.
3
FV = 250,000(1.08)^5.
4
(1.08)^5 ≈ 1.4693.
5
FV ≈ ₹367,332.
What the calculation actually means: The gain is ₹117,332—not simply 5×8%=40%. Compounding makes the base grow.
Same course, different cash-flow pattern
Problem statement

Arclight receives ₹100,000 at the end of each year for five years. Required return is 10%. What is the PV?

1
Five equal end-of-year payments means an ordinary annuity.
2
C=₹100,000; r=10%; N=5.
3
PV=(100,000/0.10)[1−1/(1.10)^5].
4
The annuity factor is ≈3.79079.
5
PV ≈ ₹379,079.
What the calculation actually means: Do not use the perpetuity formula because the payments stop after five years.
Different discount rates by year
Problem statement

Arclight will receive ₹500,000 in Year 2. The Year-1 rate is 6% and the Year-2 rate is 7%. Find PV.

1
Year 2 to Year 1 uses the 7% rate.
2
Year 1 to Year 0 uses the 6% rate.
3
PV = 500,000/(1.07×1.06).
4
PV ≈ ₹440,839.
What the calculation actually means: This is why the lecture says never average rates across periods.
Nominal and real—same answer if you stay consistent
Problem statement

A real annuity pays £100 per year for five years. The real rate is 3% and expected inflation is 2%. Find PV using the real basis.

1
Use real cash flows with a real discount rate.
2
PV=(100/0.03)[1−1/(1.03)^5].
3
PV ≈ £458.
4
Alternatively, convert the real rate to a nominal rate using Fisher and inflate the cash flows; you should get the same answer when done consistently.
What the calculation actually means: The point is consistency, not memorising two unrelated methods.

Immediate self-check

₹200,000 grows at 9% for four years. Terminal value?
Reasoning: FV=200,000(1.09)^4≈₹282,316.
A 10% nominal APR is compounded quarterly. Periodic rate?
Reasoning: 10%/4=2.5% per quarter.
At 8% annual discounting, the Year-3 discount factor is closest to?
Reasoning: 1/(1.08)^3≈0.7938.
UNIT 2

Now use time value to decide: should Arclight do the project?

Capital budgeting is TVM turned into a business decision.

Where this comes from: Unit 1 gave you PV. Capital budgeting asks you to use PV to decide whether a real project creates value.
Arclight case: Arclight can spend ₹10 lakh on a new battery line. That line will create a stream of future cash flows. Management must decide whether the project earns enough to justify tying up ₹10 lakh today.

Words you need before symbols

Initial investment (I₀)

I₀ is the cash paid at time 0—the starting investment. It is usually an outflow, so it enters NPV with a negative sign.

Cash flow (CFₜ)

CFₜ means the project’s cash flow in period t. t=1 is the first future period, t=2 the second, and so on.

Discount / hurdle rate (r)

r is the required return for a project of this risk. The course frames it as the opportunity cost of capital; if the project cannot earn the required return, it destroys value.

NPV

NPV means “net present value”: present value of future project cash flows minus what you must pay today.

IRR

IRR is a rate, not a rupee amount. It is the discount rate that makes NPV exactly zero.

PI

PI is a ratio: PV of future benefits divided by initial investment.

Payback

Payback is a time measure: how long until cumulative undiscounted cash flows recover the initial investment.

Build the idea

1. NPV is asking one clean question

“After accounting for time and risk, how many rupees of value are left above the amount I invest today?” Positive NPV means yes.

What should trigger this in your head: If you can calculate PV, you can calculate NPV: discount future cash flows, add them, subtract I₀.
Common wrong turn: NPV>0 → accept a standard investment project.
2. IRR is a return measure

IRR finds the break-even discount rate. It asks what return is embedded in the project’s cash-flow pattern.

What should trigger this in your head: If the question says “what rate makes NPV zero?”, think IRR.
Common wrong turn: For conventional investment cash flows: IRR>hurdle → accept.
3. Why IRR can fail

If cash flows change sign more than once, there can be multiple IRRs. Some projects have no IRR. Financing-type cash flows can reverse the usual rule. Mutually exclusive projects can have conflicting IRR rankings because scale and timing matter.

What should trigger this in your head: When the cash-flow pattern is weird, NPV is safer because it evaluates value at the chosen rate.
Common wrong turn: Do not mechanically pick the project with the highest IRR when projects are mutually exclusive.
4. Payback is not profitability

Payback ignores discounting and ignores everything that happens after the cutoff. It can be useful for liquidity/recovery speed but is not a value measure.

What should trigger this in your head: If the question only asks “when do we recover the ₹X?”, payback may be exactly what is wanted.
Common wrong turn: Do not interpret a shorter payback as automatically higher value.
5. PI is useful when capital is rationed

If Arclight has only ₹5 lakh available and several positive-NPV projects compete for the same money, PI helps compare value created per rupee invested.

What should trigger this in your head: PI>1 corresponds to positive NPV under the usual convention.
Common wrong turn: PI is not a universal replacement for NPV when projects are mutually exclusive.

Formula cards — read the English before the symbols

NPV
NPV = Σ[CFₜ/(1+r)^t] − I₀ — I₀ = initial outflow; CFₜ = future cash flow; r = required return; t = period.
IRR
0 = Σ[CFₜ/(1+IRR)^t] − I₀ — IRR is the rate that makes NPV zero.
PI
PI = PV(future cash flows)/I₀ — PI>1 for a standard acceptable project.
Payback
Time until cumulative undiscounted CFs recover I₀.

Worked applications: problem → reasoning → calculation → interpretation

Full NPV decision
Problem statement

Arclight invests $100,000 today. It expects $40,000 in Year 1, $50,000 in Year 2 and $60,000 in Year 3. Required return is 10%. Find NPV and decide.

1
Year 0 is −100,000.
2
PV Year 1 = 40,000/1.10 = 36,364.
3
PV Year 2 = 50,000/1.10² = 41,322.
4
PV Year 3 = 60,000/1.10³ = 45,079.
5
Total PV of inflows ≈ 122,764.
6
NPV = 122,764−100,000 = $22,764.
7
Because NPV is positive, accept.
What the calculation actually means: This is the central capital-budgeting pattern in the supplied final quiz.
Same total cash, different timing
Problem statement

Project A and Project B each cost $60,000. Each produces total cash of $70,000 over two years. A pays $50,000 in Year 1 and $20,000 in Year 2. B pays $20,000 in Year 1 and $50,000 in Year 2. At 10%, which is better?

1
A: PV=50,000/1.10 + 20,000/1.10² ≈ 63,983; NPV≈3,983.
2
B: PV=20,000/1.10 + 50,000/1.10² ≈ 59,669; NPV≈−331.
3
Choose A.
4
Why? Both generate the same undiscounted total, but A gives more of the money earlier—and earlier cash is worth more at a positive discount rate.
What the calculation actually means: This is the conceptual bridge from Unit 1: timing changes value.
Payback
Problem statement

Arclight invests $45,000 and receives $10,000, $15,000, $15,000 and $10,000 in Years 1–4. Find simple payback.

1
Cumulative after Year 1 = 10,000.
2
After Year 2 = 25,000.
3
After Year 3 = 40,000.
4
$5,000 remains at the start of Year 4.
5
Year 4 supplies $10,000, so 5,000/10,000=0.5 year.
6
Payback = 3.5 years.
What the calculation actually means: No discounting is used in simple payback.

Immediate self-check

PV future cash flows=₹900,000; I₀=₹750,000. PI?
Reasoning: 900,000/750,000=1.20.
IRR=16%, hurdle=12% for a conventional project. Decision?
Reasoning: IRR is above the hurdle.
Why can IRR fail with −1000,+2000,−1200?
Reasoning: Two sign changes can create multiple IRRs.
UNIT 3

A bond is just a stream of promised cash flows

TVM → debt cash flows → bond price → yield → duration.

Where this comes from: This is Unit 1 again, but with a real financial security. Once you can read the bond vocabulary, the price formula is simply PV.
Arclight case: Arclight needs long-term financing. It can issue bonds. An investor buys the bond because it promises coupons and repayment of face value. The market then decides what that promise is worth today.

Words you need before symbols

Face / par value

The amount the issuer repays at maturity. If face value is ₹1,000, the investor gets ₹1,000 back at maturity, assuming no default.

Coupon (C or cpn)

The periodic interest payment. If coupon rate is 6% and face value is ₹1,000, annual coupon is ₹60. In the bond formula, C/cpn = the actual coupon cash payment.

Coupon rate

Coupon rate is the coupon as a percentage of face value. It sizes the cash flow; it is not the discount rate.

YTM / r

YTM is the return/discount rate that makes the bond’s market price equal the PV of its promised cash flows. In the bond formula, r = YTM.

t / n

The number of periods to maturity. If annual coupons and five years remain, t=5. If semiannual coupons for five years, t=10.

Price (P)

The bond’s market value today—the PV of all coupons plus the face-value payment.

Build the idea

1. Bond pricing is Unit 1 in disguise

A bond pays coupons over time and returns face value at maturity. Price is just the PV of those cash flows discounted at YTM.

What should trigger this in your head: If you can draw the timeline, you can understand the formula.
Common wrong turn: Coupon creates cash flows; YTM discounts them.
2. Par, premium and discount

At par, coupon rate=YTM. If YTM rises above the coupon rate, price falls below par. If YTM falls below coupon, price rises above par.

What should trigger this in your head: Before calculating, compare coupon rate with YTM.
Common wrong turn: A bond priced above face value is a premium bond; below face is a discount bond.
3. Why price and yield move opposite

The bond’s promised cash flows are fixed. If the discount rate rises, PV of those fixed payments falls. Nothing magical is happening—the result comes straight from the PV formula.

What should trigger this in your head: When you see “YTM rises”, immediately expect “price falls”.
Common wrong turn: The relationship is inverse.
4. Semiannual bonds

Two payments per year means the annual coupon is split into two payments and the annual YTM is converted to a half-year rate. Number of periods doubles.

What should trigger this in your head: This is a unit-conversion problem.
Common wrong turn: Half the rate + twice the periods.
5. Yield curve connects rates to time

The yield curve shows rates for bonds of different maturities. Spot is today’s rate for a particular maturity. A forward rate is a future-period rate fixed today. The course distinguishes forward rates from expected future short rates because uncertainty matters.

What should trigger this in your head: The yield curve helps choose an appropriate rate for a cash flow with a particular duration.
Common wrong turn: Do not treat a forward rate as a guaranteed forecast of the future.
6. Bond risk is not only default

Even a government bond can lose market value if yields rise. Long-duration bonds are especially sensitive. “Risk-free” in the course refers to default risk, not a guaranteed market price before maturity.

What should trigger this in your head: This connects bonds directly to risk and later to duration.
Common wrong turn: Holding to maturity reduces price volatility as a realised outcome, assuming no default, but market value can still move before maturity.
7. Duration explains sensitivity

Macaulay duration is a weighted average time to receive the bond’s value. Modified duration converts that timing idea into approximate percentage price sensitivity to yield changes.

What should trigger this in your head: Longer maturity and lower coupon usually mean higher duration.
Common wrong turn: Duration is an approximation, not an exact repricing formula.

Formula cards — read the English before the symbols

Coupon cash flow
Coupon = Coupon rate × Face value
Bond price
P = C[1−1/(1+YTM)^t]/YTM + FV/(1+YTM)^t — C=coupon cash payment; FV=face value; t=years/periods; YTM=discount rate.
Current yield
Current yield = annual coupon / market price
Macaulay duration
Duration = Σ[t×PV(CFₜ)]/Price
Modified duration
MD = Duration/(1+YTM)
Price sensitivity
%ΔP ≈ −MD×ΔY — ΔY must be in decimal form.
Fisher
1+r_nominal=(1+r_real)(1+π)

Worked applications: problem → reasoning → calculation → interpretation

Full bond price
Problem statement

A 5-year bond has face value ₹1,000, annual coupon rate 6%, annual coupons and YTM 8%. Find its price.

1
Coupon = 6%×₹1,000 = ₹60.
2
PV of five ₹60 coupons = 60[1−1/(1.08)^5]/0.08 ≈ ₹239.27.
3
PV of ₹1,000 face value = 1,000/(1.08)^5 ≈ ₹680.58.
4
Price ≈ ₹239.27+₹680.58 = ₹919.85.
5
Sanity check: YTM 8%>coupon 6%, so price must be below ₹1,000.
What the calculation actually means: The exact number is less important than being able to explain why the bond trades below par.
Semiannual setup
Problem statement

A ₹1,000 bond has annual coupon rate 4.25%, 3 years to maturity, semiannual coupons and annualised YTM 0.965%. Set up the price calculation.

1
Annual coupon = 4.25%×1,000=₹42.50.
2
Semiannual coupon = ₹42.50/2=₹21.25.
3
Periods = 3×2=6.
4
Periodic YTM = 0.965%/2=0.4825%.
5
Price = PV of six ₹21.25 payments + PV of ₹1,000 at period 6.
6
Because coupon rate is far above YTM, the price should be above par.
What the calculation actually means: A good exam answer often begins with a correct setup, even before the calculator work.
Duration shock
Problem statement

A bond has modified duration 6. YTM rises by 0.50 percentage points. Approximate price change.

1
0.50 percentage point = 0.005.
2
%ΔP≈−MD×ΔY.
3
=−6×0.005.
4
≈−3.0%.
What the calculation actually means: The minus sign is the mathematical version of “yield up, price down”.

Immediate self-check

₹1,000 bond, coupon 8%, YTM 6%. Trades:
Reasoning: Coupon>YTM, so price is above par.
Lower coupon, same maturity/YTM generally means:
Reasoning: More value is concentrated in the distant face-value payment.
YTM rises by 1%. What happens to a fixed bond price?
Reasoning: Higher discounting lowers PV.
UNIT 4

Stocks use the same PV logic—but now the future is uncertain

Expected return → dividends → DCF → Gordon growth → PVGO.

Where this comes from: Bonds had promised coupons. Stocks do not. Your next step is to value uncertain shareholder cash flows.
Arclight case: Arclight is deciding whether to retain earnings for a new product or distribute them. An investor buying Arclight stock is really buying a claim on future dividends and future share value.

Words you need before symbols

D₀ and D₁

D₀ means the dividend just paid/current-period dividend. D₁ means the dividend expected next period. Gordon Growth uses D₁ because the first cash flow in the model arrives next period.

P₀ and P₁

P₀ is the stock price today. P₁ is the expected price one period from now. These are prices, not dividends.

r

r is the required/expected return for the stock. In the lecture it is also called the market capitalisation rate or cost of equity.

g

g is the expected constant dividend growth rate in the Gordon model.

E / EPS

E is earnings per share in the PVGO setup; EPS is earnings per share. They are the profit attributable to one share.

ROE

ROE = return on equity: net income divided by book equity. It tells you how effectively the firm generates earnings from shareholders’ equity.

Payout and plowback

Payout is the fraction of earnings distributed as dividends. Plowback is what is retained/reinvested: 1−payout.

Build the idea

1. What do you receive for owning a stock?

Two pieces: dividends while you own it and the price you can sell it for later. Therefore one-period expected return is dividend plus price appreciation, divided by today’s price.

What should trigger this in your head: If the question gives D₁ and P₁, it is probably a one-period return problem.
Common wrong turn: Do not forget the price change component.
2. DCF is just PV applied to dividends

At a finite horizon, today’s stock price equals the PV of dividends plus the expected selling price. Keep pushing the horizon forward and the value becomes a stream of expected shareholder cash flows.

What should trigger this in your head: This is Unit 1 again: future cash flows discounted back to today.
Common wrong turn: The discount rate must fit the stock’s required return.
3. Gordon Growth: when does the shortcut work?

If dividends grow at a constant rate forever, the infinite stream simplifies to P₀=D₁/(r−g). It only works when r>g.

What should trigger this in your head: Constant growth forever + next year dividend + required return → Gordon.
Common wrong turn: Use D₁, not D₀. Check r>g.
4. Where does g come from?

The course gives a simple sustainable-growth relationship: g = ROE×plowback. If a company retains more earnings and earns a strong return on the equity it reinvests, it can grow faster.

What should trigger this in your head: Payout and plowback are opposites.
Common wrong turn: Do not multiply ROE by payout when asked for sustainable growth.
5. PVGO: how much price is growth?

Imagine the company pays out 100% of earnings forever. Then it cannot reinvest for growth. The no-growth value is E/r. Actual price minus E/r is PVGO—the value attributed to growth opportunities.

What should trigger this in your head: PVGO connects valuation to strategy: mature firms may have little growth value; growth firms may have a large portion of price tied to future opportunities.
Common wrong turn: PVGO is not “the growth rate”. It is a rupee value.

UNIT 5

Risk explains the return you require—and why portfolios work

Volatility → correlation → diversification → beta → CAPM → Sharpe.

Where this comes from: We have been discounting cash flows using r. Now we need to understand where that required return comes from.
Arclight case: Arclight has cash to invest. It can buy Stock A or Stock B, or combine them. The company/investor cares about both expected return and uncertainty. Portfolio construction can reduce some risk because assets do not move together perfectly.

Words you need before symbols

Expected return

The return investors expect before the outcome happens. In the course, expected return is forward-looking; actual/realised return is backward-looking.

Variance / SD

Variance measures squared dispersion around the mean. σ (standard deviation) is the square root of variance and is the course’s main total-risk/volatility measure.

Weight (w)

wᵢ is the fraction of your portfolio invested in asset i. Example: 70%=0.70.

Correlation (ρ)

ρ measures how two assets move together, from −1 to +1. Low or negative correlation creates diversification benefit.

Covariance

Cov captures co-movement in the portfolio-variance formula. Cov=ρ×σ₁×σ₂ for two assets.

Beta (β)

β measures sensitivity to the market. β=1 roughly means market-level sensitivity; β>1 is more sensitive; β<1 is less sensitive.

Rf / Rm / MRP

Rf=risk-free rate. Rm=market return. MRP=market risk premium=E[Rm]−Rf.

Build the idea

1. Risk is uncertainty, not simply “bad” outcomes

The course defines risk as the effect of uncertainty on objectives. A volatile investment has outcomes spread around its expectation.

What should trigger this in your head: If a question asks for risk using a single asset measure, standard deviation is usually the target.
Common wrong turn: Do not say “higher standard deviation = worse investment” without considering return.
2. Portfolio return is easy

Expected portfolio return is the weighted average of expected returns. It does not need correlation.

What should trigger this in your head: Weights + expected returns → weighted average.
Common wrong turn: Do not use portfolio-variance machinery for expected return.
3. Portfolio risk is where the interesting part begins

Portfolio variance contains individual variance terms plus a covariance term. The covariance term captures whether assets move together.

What should trigger this in your head: If correlation falls, the covariance contribution falls and diversification improves.
Common wrong turn: Portfolio SD is generally not the weighted average of individual SDs.
4. Diversifiable vs systematic risk

Firm-specific/unique risk can largely disappear in a broad portfolio. Economy-wide/systematic risk cannot.

What should trigger this in your head: CAPM focuses on the systematic risk that diversified investors are still exposed to.
Common wrong turn: Diversification does not make market risk disappear.
5. Beta and CAPM

Beta connects an asset to market movements. CAPM then says required return = risk-free rate + beta×market risk premium.

What should trigger this in your head: If rf, market return and beta are given, compute MRP first, then beta compensation, then add rf.
Common wrong turn: Beta multiplies the market risk premium, not the entire market return.
6. Sharpe and investor choice

Sharpe is excess return per unit of total risk. For a risk-averse investor, same return with lower SD is better, and same SD with higher expected return is better.

What should trigger this in your head: When a question compares two portfolios on both return and risk, do not automatically choose the higher raw return.
Common wrong turn: Dominance and Sharpe answer different comparison questions.

UNIT 6

Derivatives become easy when you start from the exposure

Spot → forward/futures → long/short → hedging → FX.

Where this comes from: Risk told us what can go wrong. Derivatives give you a way to offset that risk by taking an opposite position.
Arclight case: Arclight will buy copper in three months. If copper prices rise, its production cost rises. A derivative can be used so that gains on the hedge offset some of the higher purchase cost.

Words you need before symbols

Spot

A spot transaction means the asset and money are exchanged now.

Forward

A forward is a privately negotiated OTC obligation to transact later at an agreed delivery price K. OTC means over-the-counter.

Futures

Futures are standardised, exchange-traded contracts cleared through a clearinghouse and marked to market. This reduces counterparty risk relative to a bespoke OTC forward.

Long / short

Long = commitment to buy; benefits when price rises. Short = commitment to sell; benefits when price falls.

S₀ / Sₜ / K / F₀ / T

S₀=spot price today. Sₜ=spot price at maturity. K=agreed delivery price. F₀=theoretical forward price today. T=time to maturity.

Hedge

A hedge is an offsetting position designed to reduce price risk in an existing exposure. The key is not guessing the market; it is reducing the sensitivity of the combined position.

Build the idea

1. Start with what hurts

If you already own inventory and plan to sell it later, a price fall hurts. If you will need to buy a commodity later, a price rise hurts.

What should trigger this in your head: This “what hurts?” question determines hedge direction.
Common wrong turn: Do not memorise “seller=short” without understanding why.
2. Forward vs futures

Both create future obligations, but forwards are bespoke OTC contracts while futures are standardised and exchange-traded with clearing and daily settlement.

What should trigger this in your head: Questions about clearing, standardisation or daily settlement are futures clues.
Common wrong turn: A forward is not an option: both parties have obligations.
3. Forward payoff

For a long forward, payoff at maturity is Sₜ−K. For a short forward, it is K−Sₜ.

What should trigger this in your head: If Sₜ>K, the long side gains. If Sₜ
Common wrong turn: Do not subtract an option premium unless the problem actually includes an option.
4. FX is the same exposure logic

Importer who must buy USD later fears USD appreciation → long USD futures. Exporter who will receive USD later fears USD depreciation → short USD futures.

What should trigger this in your head: Translate currency stories into “future buyer” or “future seller”.
Common wrong turn: The direction follows the exposure, not your personal market forecast.
5. Why hedge if it is not free money?

The course explains that hedging can reduce costly cash shortfalls, financial distress and agency costs even when the hedge itself is approximately zero-sum.

What should trigger this in your head: Risk management is about the firm’s overall cash-flow stability.
Common wrong turn: A hedge is not a guarantee of trading profit.

UNIT 7

Options, swaps and CDS: changing the shape of risk

Rights vs obligations → payoff → profit → breakeven → swaps/CDS.

Where this comes from: Forwards lock both sides into an obligation. Options are different: the buyer pays for a right and can choose not to exercise.
Arclight case: Arclight is worried a component price could rise but does not want to lose all benefit if the price falls. An option can protect one side while preserving the other side of the payoff—at the cost of a premium.

Words you need before symbols

Call

A call gives the holder the right, not obligation, to buy the underlying at strike X.

Put

A put gives the holder the right, not obligation, to sell the underlying at strike X.

S / Sₜ

S is the underlying asset price; at expiry Sₜ is the terminal price.

X

X is the strike price—the price at which the option holder can buy (call) or sell (put).

Premium

The premium is what the option buyer pays upfront for the right.

Payoff vs profit

Payoff is what the option generates at expiry. Profit subtracts the premium paid. This distinction is central to the exam questions.

Swap / notional

A plain-vanilla interest-rate swap exchanges fixed and floating interest cash flows on a notional principal. The notional is normally a reference amount, not principal that changes hands.

CDS

A credit default swap transfers credit/default-event risk to the protection seller.

Build the idea

1. Option = right, not obligation

A buyer of a call will exercise only if buying at X is better than buying at the market. A put is the mirror image.

What should trigger this in your head: The word “right” is the clue that the buyer can walk away.
Common wrong turn: Do not treat an option like a forward.
2. Intrinsic value / payoff

Call payoff=max(S−X,0). Put payoff=max(X−S,0). Negative exercise value is replaced by zero because the buyer can decline to exercise.

What should trigger this in your head: At expiry, call ITM when S>X; put ITM when S
Common wrong turn: These are payoffs, not buyer profits.
3. Profit includes premium

A call buyer’s profit is max(S−X,0)−premium. A put buyer’s profit is max(X−S,0)−premium.

What should trigger this in your head: If premium is in the question and the word “profit” appears, subtract it.
Common wrong turn: Do not confuse strike with breakeven.
4. Breakeven

Call breakeven=X+premium. Put breakeven=X−premium. The premium shifts the point where the buyer’s total profit becomes zero.

What should trigger this in your head: If the question asks “what stock price makes me break even?”, use the premium-adjusted level.
Common wrong turn: Strike alone is not breakeven.
5. Long straddle

A long straddle buys a call and a put with the same strike and maturity. It wins when the underlying moves far enough in either direction to cover both premiums.

What should trigger this in your head: Think “large move either way”.
Common wrong turn: It is not a pure bullish or bearish strategy.
6. Swaps and CDS

Swaps exchange streams of payments. In an interest-rate swap, one side can move from floating exposure toward fixed. CDS transfers credit/default risk.

What should trigger this in your head: Interest-rate words → swap. Default/credit event → CDS.
Common wrong turn: A swap does not automatically eliminate all risk.

UNIT 8

Bring everything together: the exam is one system

Valuation + risk + decision + hedging.

Where this comes from: This is where the chapters stop being chapters. A mixed MCQ is usually testing whether you can recognise where a business decision sits inside the system.
Arclight case: Arclight now has: a project to evaluate, financing to raise, stock investors to satisfy, a portfolio of financial assets to manage and future commodity/FX exposures to hedge. You already have every tool you need; the skill is choosing the right one.

Words you need before symbols

MRP

Market risk premium = expected market return − risk-free rate. It is the extra expected return for broad market risk.

Country risk premium

The supplied lecture/question bank describes a country-specific premium as additional compensation for country-specific risk relative to a base market. The lecture example lists India’s country ERP as 6.06% in its cited Damodaran 2018 example.

Dominance

For a risk-averse investor, same return + lower risk is better; same risk + higher return is better.

CAPM expected return

Rf + beta×MRP. Beta captures systematic market exposure, not diversifiable firm-specific risk.

Build the idea

1. One cash-flow engine

TVM is the underlying engine. NPV uses it on projects. Bonds use it on contractual debt cash flows. Stocks use it on dividends. The only thing that changes is the cash-flow story.

What should trigger this in your head: When a mixed question gives a future cash flow and asks for today’s value, go back to PV thinking.
Common wrong turn: If you understand the timeline, you often do not need to memorise a separate “chapter formula”.
2. Risk determines the rate

A riskier cash flow requires a higher required return. CAPM offers a framework based on systematic market exposure. That required return then becomes the discount rate in valuation.

What should trigger this in your head: This is the connection between Unit 5 and Units 2–4.
Common wrong turn: Do not treat the discount rate as a random input disconnected from risk.
3. Project, bond, stock: same skeleton

Project: discount project cash flows. Bond: discount coupons + face value. Stock: discount dividends/future price. All are PV problems.

What should trigger this in your head: Ask “what cash flows am I valuing?” before asking “which formula?”
Common wrong turn: A new chapter does not necessarily mean new mathematics.
4. Hedge is the mirror image of exposure

If a price move hurts the existing position, choose a derivative position that benefits from that move. That is the simplest way to derive futures direction.

What should trigger this in your head: This solves commodity and FX hedge questions reliably.
Common wrong turn: Do not hedge based on whether you think the asset will rise or fall; hedge based on your existing exposure.
5. Mixed MCQ classification

Most questions fall into a small set: time value, project valuation, bond pricing/yield, stock valuation, portfolio risk, CAPM, hedge direction, option payoff/profit, swaps/CDS, or simple definitions.

What should trigger this in your head: Classify first; calculate second. Some questions need no calculation at all.
Common wrong turn: If the question can be solved by one definition or sign intuition, do not overcomplicate it.
