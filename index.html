Accounting & Control from Zero

Build the language first. Then the numbers start making sense.

01
Core idea

What this course is actually about

Managerial accounting gives managers information for planning, control and decisions. Financial accounting primarily serves external users; managerial accounting is internal, more flexible in timing and detail, and is not constrained by the same reporting format.

Core idea

Cost object, cost, cost driver

A cost object is what you want to measure the cost of (product, customer, department). A cost driver is the activity that causes a cost to change. The chain is: resources → activities → costs → cost drivers → cost objects.

Core idea

Three questions to ask

1) What happened? 2) Why did it happen? 3) What should we do next? The course moves from reporting and cost behaviour into decision models, budgets and control systems.

Worked problems

Every number is shown so you can reconstruct the result rather than memorize it.
Problem
A business has total assets of €100,000 and total liabilities of €60,000. What is owners’ equity?
MCQ bank · Basic accounting
Solution
Accounting equation
1
Start with the accounting equation
Assets = Liabilities + Equity.
2
Rearrange for equity
Equity = Assets − Liabilities.
3
Insert the figures
Equity = €100,000 − €60,000 = €40,000.
Answer
€40,000
Why this is the correct result

Equity is the residual interest after subtracting liabilities from assets. The equation must still balance: €100,000 = €60,000 + €40,000.

Exam takeaway: When two sides of the accounting equation are known, solve the third by rearranging the equation.
Problem
A customer pays €10,000 in advance for a service that the company has not yet performed. Under accrual accounting, how should the receipt be treated?
MCQ bank · Accrual accounting
Solution
Revenue recognition
1
Ask whether the revenue has been earned

No. The service has not yet been performed.

2
Record what actually exists
The company has received €10,000 cash and now owes the customer the service.
3
Classify the obligation
The €10,000 is deferred (unearned) revenue, a liability, until the service is performed.
Answer
Cash +€10,000 and deferred/unearned revenue +€10,000; no revenue yet.
Why this is the correct result

Accrual accounting recognizes revenue when it is earned, not merely when cash is collected.

Exam takeaway: Cash timing and revenue timing are different under accrual accounting.
Problem
A company purchases equipment on credit for €25,000. What journal entry records the transaction?
MCQ bank · Transaction recording
Solution
Double entry
1
Identify the asset received
Equipment increases by €25,000.
2
Identify the financing source
Because the purchase is on credit, Accounts Payable increases by €25,000.
3
Apply double entry
Debit Equipment €25,000; Credit Accounts Payable €25,000.
Answer
Dr Equipment €25,000; Cr Accounts Payable €25,000.
Why this is the correct result

The company now owns more equipment and owes the supplier more money. Both sides increase, so the accounting equation stays balanced.

Exam takeaway: For a transaction, first ask what came in, what went out, and what obligation or equity changed.
Exam cues
Do not confuse revenue, cash and profit.
For “not part of the balance sheet,” think Assets + Liabilities + Equity only.
For journal entries: Assets/Expenses generally increase with debits; Liabilities/Equity/Revenue generally increase with credits.

UNIT 1 · 02

Cost Behaviour & CVP

The fastest route from raw data to break-even, target profit and operating leverage.

Learn from zeroWorked examplesExam cues

What you need to understand

Build the mental model before touching the calculator.
02
Core idea

Variable vs fixed

Variable cost changes in total with activity; variable cost per unit is normally constant within the relevant range. Fixed cost stays constant in total within the relevant range; fixed cost per unit falls as volume rises.

Core idea

Relevant range

The cost behaviour model only holds inside the activity range where the assumed relationship is valid. Step costs jump when capacity bands are exceeded; mixed costs contain both fixed and variable components.

Core idea

Contribution margin

Contribution margin = Sales − Variable costs. It is what is left to cover fixed costs and then create operating profit. Unit CM = selling price − unit variable cost.

Core idea

CVP assumptions

Linear revenue/cost relationships, constant unit variable cost, fixed costs constant within the relevant range, stable sales mix, and production approximately equal to sales.

Worked problems

Every number is shown so you can reconstruct the result rather than memorize it.
Problem
Company A sells 5,000 units at $10 each. Variable cost is $4 per unit and fixed cost is $5 per unit. What is the contribution margin per unit?
CVP Quiz · Company A, Q1
Solution
Contribution margin
1
Separate variable from fixed cost

Contribution margin uses selling price minus variable cost. Fixed cost is not part of unit CM.

2
Compute unit contribution margin
CM/unit = $10 − $4 = $6.
3
Check the distractors
$5 is the stated fixed cost; $9 ignores the variable cost; $1 is not the contribution margin.
Answer
$6 per unit
Why this is the correct result

Each unit first contributes $6 toward fixed costs and profit; only the $4 variable cost is deducted from the $10 selling price.

Exam takeaway: CVP starts with contribution margin, not gross margin or fixed cost.
Problem
Company C sells for $24 per unit, has variable cost of $6 per unit, and total fixed costs of $72,000. How many units must it sell to break even?
CVP Quiz · Company C, Q3
Solution
Break-even
1
Find unit contribution margin
CM/unit = $24 − $6 = $18.
2
Use the break-even formula
BEP units = Fixed costs ÷ CM/unit.
3
Calculate
BEP = $72,000 ÷ $18 = 4,000 units.
4
Verify
4,000 × $18 = $72,000, exactly covering fixed costs, so operating income is zero.
Answer
4,000 units
Why this is the correct result

At break-even, total contribution margin exactly equals total fixed costs.

Exam takeaway: Memorize the logic: fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit.
Problem
Company D sells for $32 per unit. Variable manufacturing cost is $18 per unit and variable non-manufacturing cost is $6 per unit. Fixed manufacturing costs are $70,000 and fixed non-manufacturing costs are $26,000. How many units are required to break even?
Mock Exam IMB 6 · Q5
Solution
Break-even with manufacturing + non-manufacturing costs
1
Combine all variable costs relevant to the contribution calculation
Variable cost/unit = $18 + $6 = $24.
2
Calculate contribution per unit
CM/unit = $32 − $24 = $8.
3
Combine all fixed costs
Fixed costs = $70,000 + $26,000 = $96,000.
4
Calculate break-even volume
BEP = $96,000 ÷ $8 = 12,000 units.
5
Verify
12,000 × $8 = $96,000, so operating income = $0.
Answer
12,000 units
Why this is the correct result

Both manufacturing and non-manufacturing costs listed as variable affect contribution; both fixed-cost pools must be covered at break-even.

Exam takeaway: Do not ignore non-manufacturing variable or fixed costs when the question includes them in the CVP model.
Problem
Company G sells for $8 per unit, has variable cost of $3 per unit and fixed costs of $20,000. How many units must it sell to earn a $30,000 profit?
CVP Quiz · Company G, Q7
Solution
Target profit
1
Find unit contribution margin
CM/unit = $8 − $3 = $5.
2
Set the contribution requirement
The company must cover $20,000 fixed costs and then generate $30,000 profit: required CM = $50,000.
3
Solve for units
Units = ($20,000 + $30,000) ÷ $5 = 10,000 units.
4
Verify
10,000 × $5 = $50,000; $50,000 − $20,000 = $30,000 profit.
Answer
10,000 units
Why this is the correct result

Target-profit CVP adds the target profit to fixed costs before dividing by unit contribution margin.

Exam takeaway: Target profit formula: (Fixed costs + target operating profit) ÷ CM/unit.
Exam cues
Never subtract fixed cost to get unit contribution margin.
For after-tax target profit: convert the desired after-tax profit into the pre-tax equivalent first.
For multi-product questions: compute weighted/average CM using the stated sales mix before dividing fixed costs.

UNIT 2 · 03

Cost Management: Traditional Costing → ABC

Understand why overhead allocation can distort product economics—and how ABC fixes the signal.

Learn from zeroWorked examplesExam cues

What you need to understand

Build the mental model before touching the calculator.
03
Core idea

Manufacturing cost building blocks

Direct materials, direct labor and manufacturing overhead make up product manufacturing cost. Direct costs can be traced economically to a cost object; indirect costs need allocation.

Core idea

Traditional allocation

A plantwide rate uses one broad allocation base; departmental rates use separate rates. Both can misassign overhead when products consume support resources differently.

Core idea

ABC logic

ABC first allocates resource costs to activities, then activity costs to products using cause-and-effect cost drivers. Typical drivers: number of setups, material pounds handled, purchase orders, inspections, machine hours.

Core idea

ABC is not “more accurate” by magic

ABC adds value when products/customers consume support activities differently and the benefit of better information exceeds the cost of designing/maintaining the system.

Core idea

ABM

Activity-based management uses ABC information to improve operations and strategy: reduce non-value-added activities, redesign processes, change product mix, or rethink customers/channels.

Worked problems

Every number is shown so you can reconstruct the result rather than memorize it.
Problem
Company E expects sales of 12,000 units, has variable cost of $3 per unit and fixed costs of $60,000. What selling price per unit would produce break-even at 12,000 units?
CVP Quiz · Company E, Q5
Solution
Break-even selling price
1
Find required total contribution
At break-even, contribution margin must equal fixed costs: $60,000.
2
Find required CM per unit
$60,000 ÷ 12,000 = $5 CM per unit.
3
Add variable cost back
Selling price = Variable cost + CM = $3 + $5 = $8.
4
Verify
At $8 price, CM/unit = $8 − $3 = $5; 12,000 × $5 = $60,000.
Answer
$8 per unit
Why this is the correct result

The selling price must generate exactly enough contribution per unit to cover the fixed-cost requirement at the forecast volume.

Exam takeaway: When the unknown is price, solve backwards from the required contribution per unit.
Problem
Company K sells Basic at $200 with $120 variable cost and Deluxe at $250 with $150 variable cost. The typical mix is 70% Basic and 30% Deluxe. Fixed costs are $86,000. How many Basic and Deluxe units are needed to break even?
CVP Quiz · Company K, Q10
Solution
Multi-product CVP
1
Compute each model’s contribution
Basic CM = $200 − $120 = $80; Deluxe CM = $250 − $150 = $100.
2
Build one composite sales bundle

A 70/30 mix over 10 units means 7 Basic + 3 Deluxe.

3
Compute bundle contribution
7×$80 + 3×$100 = $860 contribution per 10-unit bundle.
4
Find bundles at break-even
$86,000 ÷ $860 = 100 bundles.
5
Convert bundles to units
Basic = 100×7 = 700; Deluxe = 100×3 = 300.
6
Verify
700×$80 + 300×$100 = $86,000, so break-even is reached.
Answer
700 Basic units and 300 Deluxe units
Why this is the correct result

The sales mix must be preserved while the weighted contribution of the mix covers the fixed-cost pool.

Exam takeaway: For multi-product CVP, use a composite bundle or weighted-average contribution margin.
Problem
Goldman has machine-setup costs of $180,000 for 1,500 setup hours, materials-handling costs of $50,000 for 12,500 pounds, and electric-power costs of $20,000 for 20,000 kWh. Product A uses 100 setup hours, 500 pounds and 1,000 kWh, produces 4,000 units, and has $20,000 direct materials plus $12,000 direct labour. Using ABC, what is Product A’s total production cost per unit?
Mock Exam IMB 6 · Q6
Solution
Activity-based costing
1
Calculate the setup rate
$180,000 ÷ 1,500 = $120 per setup hour.
2
Calculate the materials-handling rate
$50,000 ÷ 12,500 = $4 per pound.
3
Calculate the power rate
$20,000 ÷ 20,000 = $1 per kWh.
4
Assign setup cost to A
100 × $120 = $12,000.
5
Assign handling cost to A
500 × $4 = $2,000.
6
Assign power cost to A
1,000 × $1 = $1,000.
7
Total indirect cost assigned to A
$12,000 + $2,000 + $1,000 = $15,000.
8
Add direct costs
$20,000 + $12,000 + $15,000 = $47,000 total production cost.
9
Convert to a unit cost
$47,000 ÷ 4,000 = $11.75 per unit.
Answer
$11.75 per unit
Why this is the correct result

ABC first creates a rate for each activity pool, then assigns indirect cost based on the driver actually consumed by Product A. Direct materials and direct labour are added separately.

Exam takeaway: ABC = activity-pool cost ÷ total driver → apply rate to the product → add direct costs.
Exam cues
Read the wording: “manufacturing cost” excludes selling/administrative costs.
Calculate the activity rate on the TOTAL driver before allocating to a product.
Then add direct costs if the question asks for total production cost per unit.

UNIT 3 · 04

Relevant Information & Operational Decisions

The core decision rule: ignore sunk costs; include future differences and opportunity costs.

Learn from zeroWorked examplesExam cues

What you need to understand

Build the mental model before touching the calculator.
04
Core idea

Relevant cost

A future cost/revenue that differs between alternatives. Historical/sunk costs are irrelevant because they cannot be changed by today’s choice.

Core idea

Differential / incremental / outlay / opportunity

Differential = difference between alternatives. Incremental = additional cost/benefit from the proposed option. Outlay = cash disbursement. Opportunity cost = best contribution forgone when a constrained resource is used elsewhere.

Core idea

Make or buy

Compare only avoidable manufacturing costs + opportunity costs against the purchase price. Unavoidable fixed costs stay out of the decision.

Core idea

Drop/add segment

Allocated common costs are usually irrelevant if they remain after the segment is dropped. Focus on contribution lost and avoidable costs saved.

Core idea

Scarce resource

Rank products by contribution margin per unit of the scarce resource—not by CM per product unit.

Core idea

Joint products / split-off

Joint costs incurred before split-off are sunk for the sell-vs-process-further decision. Compare incremental revenue after further processing with incremental processing cost.

Core idea

Replace equipment

Old equipment acquisition/book value is sunk. New purchase cost, disposal value of old equipment, and future operating-cost differences are relevant.

Worked problems

Every number is shown so you can reconstruct the result rather than memorize it.
Problem
STYLE s.p.a. has six activities: new-product design (€350,000; 14 projects), sales-force management (€960,000; 12 agents), training (€240,000; 48 training days), warehouse management (€120,000; 4,000 sqm), outbound logistics (€180,000; 3,000 stock movements), and order management (€261,000; 29,000 orders). Show the activity rates and the indirect costs assigned to Showers and Faucets.
STYLE s.p.a. (ABC) · supplied solution
Solution
ABC with multiple activity pools
1
Compute each activity rate
Design: €350,000÷14 = €25,000/project. Sales-force management: €960,000÷12 = €80,000/agent. Training: €240,000÷48 = €5,000/day. Warehouse: €120,000÷4,000 = €30/sqm. Outbound: €180,000÷3,000 = €60/movement. Orders: €261,000÷29,000 = €9/order.
2
Apply the rates to Showers
Warehouse 2,500×€30=€75,000; Training 30×€5,000=€150,000; Outbound 1,200×€60=€72,000; Design 6×€25,000=€150,000; Orders 7,500×€9=€67,500; Sales-force 5×€80,000=€400,000.
3
Sum Showers
€75,000 + €150,000 + €72,000 + €150,000 + €67,500 + €400,000 = €914,500.
4
Apply the rates to Faucets
Warehouse 1,500×€30=€45,000; Training 18×€5,000=€90,000; Outbound 1,800×€60=€108,000; Design 8×€25,000=€200,000; Orders 21,500×€9=€193,500; Sales-force 7×€80,000=€560,000.
5
Sum Faucets
€45,000 + €90,000 + €108,000 + €200,000 + €193,500 + €560,000 = €1,196,500.
Answer
Showers: €914,500; Faucets: €1,196,500 of indirect costs assigned.
Why this is the correct result

Each pool follows its own cause-and-effect driver; the assignment is therefore different from simply spreading all indirect cost by volume.

Exam takeaway: The hardest ABC step is choosing/using the correct driver for each activity, not the arithmetic.
Problem
Company X plans to produce 20,000 products. The exercise states a standard direct-material cost of €50 based on 5 kg per product, direct labour of 2 hours at €25/hour, variable indirect production costs of €0.20 per € of material used, and fixed indirect production costs of €200,000 allocated on direct-labour hours. Build the standard cost per product.
STANDARD COST EXERCISE · supplied exercise
Solution
Standard cost sheet
1
Direct materials
Given standard DM cost = €50 per product. With 5 kg, the implied standard material price is €10/kg.
2
Direct labour
2 hours × €25 = €50 per product.
3
Variable manufacturing overhead
€0.20 per € of material cost means 20% × €50 = €10 per product.
4
Fixed manufacturing overhead rate
Total DL hours = 20,000 products × 2 hours = 40,000 hours. Rate = €200,000 ÷ 40,000 = €5/DL hour.
5
Fixed overhead per product
2 hours × €5 = €10.
6
Total standard cost
€50 DM + €50 DL + €10 variable OH + €10 fixed OH = €120 per product.
Answer
€120 standard manufacturing cost per product.
Why this is the correct result

The exercise’s €50 figure is used as the standard direct-material cost; the other components are derived from the explicitly stated quantities and rates.

Exam takeaway: A standard cost sheet is built component-by-component: DM + DL + variable OH + fixed OH.
Problem
BMW needs 200,000 engine components. Current direct material is €5,000,000, direct labour €1,900,000, variable OH €1,100,000 and fixed OH €3,000,000. If BMW makes, DM cost rises 10%. If BMW buys at €50/unit, 30% of fixed OH is avoided and 70% remains. Which option is better?
Mock Exam IMB 6 · Q7
Solution
Make or buy
1
Identify relevant make costs
DM, DL, variable OH and avoidable fixed OH change between alternatives. The unavoidable 70% of fixed OH does not.
2
Update direct material for making
€5,000,000 × 1.10 = €5,500,000.
3
Keep the other variable costs
DL = €1,900,000; variable OH = €1,100,000.
4
Calculate avoidable fixed OH
30% × €3,000,000 = €900,000.
5
Relevant cost to make
€5,500,000 + €1,900,000 + €1,100,000 + €900,000 = €9,400,000.
6
Cost to buy
200,000 × €50 = €10,000,000.
7
Compare
Making is €600,000 cheaper than buying, so BMW should make.
8
Check the trap
The €2,100,000 unavoidable fixed OH (70%) is not included because it remains under either alternative.
Answer
Make; relevant make cost €9.4m versus buy cost €10.0m.
Why this is the correct result

Only differential costs belong in the make-or-buy comparison. Avoidable fixed cost is relevant; unavoidable fixed cost is not.

Exam takeaway: Make-or-buy is a differential-cost problem, not a comparison of reported full costs.
Problem
Circuit Booster has 50,000 machine hours available. Standard sells for $60, has variable cost of $21 and uses 1 machine hour per unit. Premium sells for $70, has variable cost of $25 and uses 1.5 machine hours per unit. Demand is 35,000 Standard and 25,000 Premium. Which product should receive the scarce machine capacity?
Optimal Use of Scarse Resources · supplied solution
Solution
Scarce resource / product mix
1
Identify the limiting factor

Machine hours are scarce: only 50,000 are available.

2
Compute contribution per unit
Standard: $60−$21 = $39. Premium: $70−$25 = $45.
3
Convert to contribution per machine hour
Standard: $39÷1 = $39/hour. Premium: $45÷1.5 = $30/hour.
4
Rank by contribution per scarce hour
Standard ranks first because $39/hour > $30/hour.
5
Use Standard demand first

Produce all 35,000 Standard units → 35,000 hours used.

6
Allocate the remaining capacity
50,000−35,000 = 15,000 hours remain. Premium needs 1.5 hours/unit → 15,000÷1.5 = 10,000 Premium units.
7
Final mix
35,000 Standard + 10,000 Premium = 50,000 machine hours.
Answer
Produce 35,000 Standard and 10,000 Premium units.
Why this is the correct result

The scarce resource should be allocated to the product generating the greatest contribution per constrained machine hour, not the highest contribution per unit.

Exam takeaway: Under a bottleneck: rank CM per unit of scarce resource.
Problem
An old machine has a historical cost of $10,000, accumulated depreciation of $6,000, book value of $4,000, current disposal value of $2,500 and four years of remaining life. A replacement costs $8,000 and has annual cash operating costs of $3,000 versus $5,000 for the old machine. Should the company replace it?
SS 04 · Keep or replace old machine
Solution
Relevant cost: equipment replacement
1
Separate sunk cost from future cash flows
The old machine’s $10,000 historical cost and $4,000 book value are sunk and do not change with today’s decision.
2
Calculate keep-case future cash operating cost
4 years × $5,000 = $20,000.
3
Calculate replace-case future cash flows
New machine $8,000 + (4×$3,000) operating cost − $2,500 disposal proceeds = $17,500.
4
Compare incremental future cost
Replace costs $17,500 versus $20,000 to keep → replacement is $2,500 better.
5
Explain the book value trap
Writing off the $4,000 book value may appear in the accounting comparison, but it is not a future cash flow and cannot change the decision.
Answer
Replace; future cash cost is lower by $2,500.
Why this is the correct result

Only future costs and benefits that differ between alternatives are relevant. Historical cost and book value are sunk.

Exam takeaway: A cost can appear in the accounts and still be irrelevant to the decision.
Exam cues
The fastest test: “Will this cost/revenue be different because of the decision?”
Avoidable fixed cost = relevant; unavoidable fixed cost = irrelevant.
When a scarce resource exists, divide CM by the constrained resource usage.

UNIT 4 · 05

Marketing Decisions, Income Statements & Pricing

Use contribution thinking for short-run decisions; use full costing intelligently for long-run pricing.

Learn from zeroWorked examplesExam cues

What you need to understand

Build the mental model before touching the calculator.
05
Core idea

Absorption vs contribution statement

Absorption emphasizes gross margin: Sales − COGS = Gross Margin. Contribution format emphasizes variable/fixed behaviour: Sales − Variable costs = CM; CM − Fixed costs = Operating income.

Core idea

Special orders

With idle capacity, accept if incremental revenue exceeds incremental cost and qualitative constraints are satisfied. Normal fixed manufacturing cost that does not change is irrelevant.

Core idea

Pricing depends on the market

Perfect competition, imperfect competition, customer demand, competitors, legal limits and price elasticity all matter. Accounting can inform cost floors and consequences but does not “set the market” by itself.

Core idea

Cost-plus approaches

Prices may be based on variable manufacturing cost, total variable cost, full cost or total manufacturing cost plus a markup. The correct markup base matters.

Worked problems

Every number is shown so you can reconstruct the result rather than memorize it.
Problem
A joint process costs $100,000 and produces X (1 million litres, selling value $90,000 at split-off) and Y (500,000 litres, selling value $30,000 at split-off). Management is considering further processing Y. What information is needed to make the sell-or-process-further decision?
SS 04 · Joint product example
Solution
Joint products / split-off
1
Identify the split-off point
At split-off, the joint process has produced Y with a current sell value of $30,000.
2
Classify the joint processing cost
The $100,000 joint cost was incurred before the split-off decision and is therefore common to the alternatives.
3
Ask what changes after split-off

The decision depends on the additional revenue from processing Y further and the additional processing cost after split-off.

4
State the incremental test

Process further if incremental revenue > incremental processing cost; otherwise sell at split-off.

5
Identify what cannot be used alone
The $100,000 joint cost should not be allocated to Y and then treated as a new avoidable cost in the sell/process-further decision.
Answer
You need the additional selling value from processing Y further and the additional post-split-off processing cost. The $100,000 joint cost is not relevant to the post-split-off choice.
Why this is the correct result

Sell/process-further is another differential-analysis problem. Only costs and revenues that change after split-off affect the choice.

Exam takeaway: Joint cost before split-off is sunk for the post-split-off decision.
Problem
Cordell normally manufactures 1,000,000 seat covers at a total manufacturing cost of $30,000,000. It receives a special order for 100,000 units at $26 each. The order uses idle capacity, does not change regular business or fixed costs, and only variable manufacturing cost of $24 per unit changes. Should Cordell accept?
SS 04.2 · Cordell special order
Solution
Special order
1
Find incremental revenue
100,000 × $26 = $2,600,000.
2
Find incremental variable manufacturing cost
100,000 × $24 = $2,400,000.
3
Compute incremental profit
$2,600,000 − $2,400,000 = $200,000.
4
Check the assumptions

The order uses idle capacity, creates no extra fixed cost, no extra variable selling/admin cost, and does not disrupt the regular market.

UNIT 7 · 07

Flexible Budgets, Standards & Variances

Separate the effect of volume from the effect of resource control.

Learn from zeroWorked examplesExam cues

What you need to understand

Build the mental model before touching the calculator.
07
Core idea

Static vs flexible

Static budget is based on planned volume. Flexible budget restates the budget at the actual activity level. That makes cost-control evaluation fairer.

Core idea

Two variance layers

Master vs flexible = volume/activity variance. Flexible vs actual = flexible-budget variance, which reflects spending/efficiency differences at the achieved activity level.

Core idea

Standards

Ideal standards assume no human limitations or operating delays. Practical standards allow normal waste, downtime and realistic conditions.

Core idea

Standard cost

Unit standard cost = standard input quantity × standard input price. A standard cost sheet states standard quantity, standard price and standard cost for each input.

Core idea

Material & labour variance formulas

DM price = (AP−SP)×AQ; DM quantity = (AQ−SQ)×SP. DL rate = (AR−SR)×AH; DL efficiency = (AH−SH)×SR. For variable OH, apply the same rate/efficiency logic using the chosen activity base. Fixed-OH spending = Actual fixed OH − Budgeted fixed OH.

Core idea

F/U convention

For costs: actual < standard/flexible budget → generally F; actual > standard/flexible budget → U. Always explain the business cause, not just the sign.

Worked problems

Every number is shown so you can reconstruct the result rather than memorize it.
Problem
MIR budgets 10,000 units at €180 each. Actual sales are €1,440,000 and the selling price remains €180, so actual volume is 8,000 units. Standard variable cost per unit is €40 (€10 materials + €25 labour + €5 electricity). Fixed overhead is €400,000 and G&A is €500,000. Build the flexible budget at actual volume.
MIR Flexible Budget Exercise · supplied case
Solution
Flexible budget
1
Translate actual sales revenue into units
€1,440,000 ÷ €180 = 8,000 units.
2
Revenue at actual volume
8,000 × €180 = €1,440,000.
3
Variable costs at actual volume
Materials 8,000×€10=€80,000; labour 8,000×€25=€200,000; electricity 8,000×€5=€40,000. Total = €320,000.
4
Contribution margin
€1,440,000 − €320,000 = €1,120,000.
5
Add fixed costs
Fixed OH €400,000 + G&A €500,000.
6
Flexible-budget operating income
€1,120,000 − €400,000 − €500,000 = €220,000.
Answer
Flexible-budget operating income = €220,000 at 8,000 units.
Why this is the correct result

A flexible budget resets volume-sensitive budget lines to the actual activity level while keeping the budgeted rates/relationships.

Exam takeaway: Flexible budget = what the budget would have looked like at the actual volume.

Responsibility Accounting, Segments & the Balanced Scorecard

Good control asks whether the manager could actually influence the result.

Learn from zeroWorked examplesExam cues

What you need to understand

08
Core idea

Management control system

A control system links goals, measures, reporting, feedback and incentives. Good measures drive goal congruence rather than local optimisation.

Core idea

Responsibility centres

Cost centre controls costs; revenue centre controls revenues; profit centre controls revenues and costs; investment centre additionally controls/has influence over investment in assets/working capital.

Core idea

Controllability

Evaluate managers on costs/events they can meaningfully influence. A segment’s economic profitability can still be evaluated separately from a manager’s controllable performance.

Segment income statement

A useful format separates contribution margin, controllable fixed costs, contribution controllable by manager, other traceable fixed costs, segment contribution and unallocated corporate costs.

Core idea

Balanced Scorecard

Four perspectives: Financial; Customer; Internal Business Process; Learning & Growth. A good scorecard creates a cause-and-effect chain from capabilities/processes → customer outcomes → financial outcomes.

UNIT 10 · 09

ROI, Economic Profit / Residual Income & Performance

Understand why ROI can create bad incentives—and why residual income can improve goal congruence.

Learn from zeroWorked examplesExam cues

What you need to understand

Build the mental model before touching the calculator.
09
Core idea

ROI

ROI = Operating income / Invested capital. It can be decomposed: ROI = Return on Sales × Capital Turnover, where ROS = Income/Revenue and Capital Turnover = Revenue/Invested Capital.

Core idea

Why ROI helps

It normalizes performance for segment size, making divisions more comparable than absolute income alone.

Core idea

ROI trap

A manager may reject a project that earns more than the cost of capital if that project lowers the manager’s existing ROI percentage. This is dysfunctional if the project increases shareholder value.

Core idea

Residual / economic profit

Economic profit (RI) = NOPAT − Capital Charge. Capital charge = cost of capital × invested capital. Positive economic profit means the segment earns more than the capital’s required return.

Worked problems

Every number is shown so you can reconstruct the result rather than memorize it.
Problem
The exercise gives: Division X invested capital $1,050,000, revenue $2,310,000 and ROS 3%; Division Y income $142,800, revenue $2,856,000 and ROIC 10.5%; Division Z invested capital $1,200,000, income $210,000 and capital turnover 3.5. Fill the missing ROI information and compute economic profit using a 10% capital charge.
ROI and Residual Income Exercise · supplied case
Solution
ROI and economic profit
1
Division X income
$2,310,000 × 3% = $69,300.
2
Division X capital turnover and ROI
$2,310,000 ÷ $1,050,000 = 2.2; ROI = 3% × 2.2 = 6.6%.
3
Division X economic profit
$69,300 − (10%×$1,050,000) = −$35,700.
4
Division Y invested capital
ROIC = Income ÷ Invested capital → invested capital = $142,800 ÷ 10.5% = $1,360,000.
5
Division Y ROS and turnover
ROS = $142,800 ÷ $2,856,000 = 5%; turnover = $2,856,000 ÷ $1,360,000 = 2.1.
6
Division Y economic profit
$142,800 − (10%×$1,360,000) = $6,800.
7

Revenue = $1,200,000×3.5 = $4,200,000; ROS = $210,000÷$4,200,000 = 5%; ROI = $210,000÷$1,200,000 = 17.5%.
8

$210,000 − (10%×$1,200,000) = $90,000.
9

Why this is the correct result

ROI measures return as a percentage of invested capital, while economic profit/residual income asks how much remains after charging the division for the capital it uses.

Exam takeaway: ROI = ROS × capital turnover; economic profit = operating income − capital charge.
Problem
Using the completed X/Y/Z table, Division X has ROI 6.6%, Y has 10.5%, and Z has 17.5%, while the required capital return is 10%. Which divisions create positive economic profit, and why is Z clearly stronger than X?

For RI, subtract the capital charge—not the invested capital itself.

Transfer Pricing

Included here so the resource remains faithful to the full course material. Latest exam update: this topic is excluded from the exam program.

Reference onlyDo not prioritise for the exam
What it is

Internal price between responsibility centres

Transfer pricing determines the value assigned when one division of a decentralised organisation supplies another division. The management-control issue is not merely “what price looks fair”; it is how the price affects divisional performance, motivation and overall company goal congruence.

Decision lens

Think in opportunity cost

When capacity is idle, the supplying division may have little or no opportunity cost. When capacity is constrained, the minimum acceptable transfer price can include the contribution margin forgone on the best alternative use.

Typical logicMinimum transfer price = variable cost + opportunity cost; maximum = buying division’s best external alternative, subject to the specific structure in the question.
